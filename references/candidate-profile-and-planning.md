# Candidate Profile, Motivation, and Career Planning

Read this reference after the user's evidence bank, resume, or other source files have been loaded and before personalized JD analysis begins.

## Candidate-profile synthesis

Build a concise working profile from the user's own materials. Do not replace the full evidence bank. Cover only supported information:

- current education or career stage;
- experience inventory and relationship type;
- strongest verified capabilities and their evidence anchors;
- industries, business models, products, user groups, channels, and tools previously encountered;
- measurable outcomes and defensible project stories;
- location, timing, language, work-authorization, availability, travel, and other application constraints when relevant;
- target-role, industry, company, learning, compensation, location, or lifestyle preferences the user has actually stated;
- unresolved conflicts, stale facts, weak ownership boundaries, and major evidence gaps;
- current career hypotheses, clearly labeled as user-stated, assistant-inferred, or still unknown.

The profile may be saved as `candidate-profile.md` when the user wants portability. It is derived from the user's evidence and must not become a second competing factual source.

## Readiness gate

A personalized JD analysis can begin when there is enough information to identify:

1. the user's current stage and hard constraints;
2. the main experience inventory and relationship types;
3. evidence for the most relevant capabilities;
4. important target preferences or unknowns;
5. the source and confidence of material claims.

Do not demand perfect completeness. Ask only for missing information that could change eligibility, fit, motivation, career direction, or resume positioning. If the profile remains incomplete, proceed only with clearly scoped limitations.

## Candidate understanding of the industry and role

After external research, help the user form a defensible point of view rather than giving them generic industry prose. Separate:

- sourced facts about the market, company, products, users, and role;
- assistant synthesis and inference;
- the user's prior experience and observations;
- the candidate's proposed view, which requires confirmation.

The resulting view should explain the industry's development stage, value chain or business model, major changes, key constraints, what those changes mean for the role, and two or three insights the candidate can defend with evidence.

Role understanding should explain the role's user or customer, business problem, operating loop, decision rights, stakeholders, outputs, KPIs, and how the work creates product, user, revenue, efficiency, risk, or brand value.

## Motivation

Draft motivation only after the candidate profile and role intelligence are available. Connect:

- why this industry, company, product, team, and role;
- which prior experiences make the transition credible;
- what the candidate can contribute now;
- what the candidate wants to learn next;
- known gaps and a realistic plan to close them.

Do not fabricate passion, loyalty, long-term commitment, or personal values. Label a motivation statement as a proposed narrative until the user confirms it.

## Career planning

Create a realistic capability path, not a promise to remain at one company. When supported, distinguish:

- **near term:** the work, business context, and capabilities the candidate wants to establish in the next role;
- **medium term:** the broader problems, ownership, or specialization the candidate wants to grow toward;
- **development plan:** specific gaps to close through work exposure, learning, portfolio evidence, tools, or domain knowledge.

Tie the plan to the user's actual preferences and trajectory. If the user has not expressed a direction, offer two or three plausible paths with trade-offs and ask them to choose rather than inventing one definitive plan.
